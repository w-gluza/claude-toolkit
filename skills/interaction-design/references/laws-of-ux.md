# Interaction models: use and limits

These foundations have different evidential status. Experimental models, perceptual principles and design maxims are not interchangeable. The application examples below are toolkit recommendations, not experimental conclusions about this product.

## Fitts: acquiring a target

Consider movement distance and effective target width along the movement direction. Repeated small, distant controls deserve investigation. Example: enlarge the interactive area of a frequently used icon without crowding adjacent actions. Frequency helps prioritise review; it is not a variable in the basic model. Do not infer exact completion times or apply mouse results unchanged to touch, keyboard or assistive input.

Source: HCI researcher I. Scott MacKenzie's [review of Fitts' law](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/ijhcs2004.html), including original research references.

## Hick-Hyman: choosing a response

Choice reaction time depends on information and choice probabilities under the studied conditions. In interfaces, inspect ambiguous labels and unnecessarily competing decisions. Grouping or sequencing may help, but hiding options can add search and memory costs. Do not turn option count into a universal navigation limit or predict total task time from it.

Source: MacKenzie's [motor behaviour models](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/mackenzie_chapter.html), discussing Hick (1952) and Hyman (1953). This is a research synthesis, not the original experiments.

## Gestalt: perceived relationships

Proximity and similarity can imply that items belong together. Example: place help nearer its own field than the next field; ensure identical styling does not imply identical behaviour when actions differ. Check competing grouping cues and content meaning.

Source: [NN/G on proximity](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/gestalt-proximity/), a secondary application of perceptual principles.

## Jakob: familiar expectations

Check useful conventions before introducing an unfamiliar pattern. Preserve a familiar label or interaction when innovation adds learning cost without a task benefit. Familiarity depends on audience and platform; it is not a reason to copy harmful conventions or another brand's appearance.

Source: [NN/G on consistency and standards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/consistency-and-standards/).

## Tesler: allocating complexity

Identify which complexity belongs to the task and who must handle it. Example: calculate a total for the user while keeping its assumptions inspectable. Hiding required information may transfer work to memory or support. Treat conservation of complexity as a design maxim, not a measurable proof that every system has a fixed complexity budget.

Attribution: [Larry Tesler's site](https://www.nomodes.com/), which identifies his conservation-of-complexity principle.
